Step-by-step · NYC Home Purchase

How to actually
buy a home in NYC

From "thinking about it" to keys in hand — the full process, NYC-specific.

Buying in NYC is more complex than most cities. You're likely dealing with co-ops or condos (with boards, extra paperwork, and approval processes), or a single-family home in the outer boroughs. The process typically takes 3–6 months from serious search to closing.

This guide covers buying a single-family home in NYC — common in Queens, Brooklyn, the Bronx, and Staten Island. The process is most similar to buying anywhere in the US, without co-op board approval or condo rules on top.

Property types explained
Single-family
You own the land and structure outright. Common in Queens, Brooklyn, Bronx, and Staten Island. Most similar to buying elsewhere in the US.
Mortgage recording tax1.8%
Title insurance~$1,200
Monthly costsP&I + tax + ins
Flip tax on saleNone
Condo
You own your unit plus a share of common areas. More common in Manhattan. No board approval typically, but monthly common charges apply.
Mortgage recording tax1.8%
Title insurance~$1,200
Monthly costsP&I + tax + ins + common
Flip tax on saleRare
Co-op
You buy shares in a corporation that owns the building. ~75% of NYC apartments. Board approval required. Different closing costs and tax structure.
Mortgage recording tax$0
Title insurance$0
Monthly costsP&I + maint (incl. tax)
Flip tax on sale1-3%
Key co-op difference: You don't technically own real estate — you own shares in a corporation. This means no mortgage recording tax (~$10K savings on a $550K loan), no title insurance (~$1,200 savings), but you'll face board approval and potential flip tax when selling. Co-op maintenance also includes property tax, so it appears higher but is comprehensive.
The full process
1
Phase 1 · Before you search
Get your finances in order

Before you look at a single listing, know your numbers cold. Lenders look at your credit score, debt-to-income ratio (DTI), employment history, and savings. NYC homes move fast — arriving unprepared means losing houses to other buyers.

Pull your credit report (free at annualcreditreport.com), pay down revolving debt to reduce DTI, and don't open any new credit accounts for 6 months before applying.

Credit score 700+ ideal DTI below 43% 2 yrs employment history 3–6 months of reserves
NYC specific: With median prices $950K+, even a conventional loan at 5% down means ~$48K down on a $950K home. Most lenders prefer 20% to avoid PMI, which would be $190K. Factor this into your savings timeline.
2
Phase 1 · Before you search
Get pre-approved for a mortgage

Pre-approval is different from pre-qualification. Pre-approval involves actually submitting your tax returns, W-2s, bank statements, and pay stubs to a lender, who then gives you a conditional commitment letter. In NYC, sellers won't take you seriously without it.

Shop at least 3 lenders — rates vary more than people expect. Consider local credit unions (like NYFCU or Teachers FCU) alongside big banks; they often have better rates for outer-borough buyers.

Locks in your budget ceiling Valid ~60–90 days Shop 3+ lenders
Credit check per lender$0–$50
Hard inquiries (multiple in 45 days count as 1)~5 pt credit dip
3
Phase 1 · Before you search
Hire a buyer's attorney — before you make an offer

In NYC, you must have an attorney to buy real estate. Unlike most states, there are no "escrow officers" or "title companies" running the deal — attorneys manage everything from contract review to closing. Hire yours before you need them.

Your attorney reviews the contract of sale, title history, open permits, violations, and coordinates with the seller's attorney. Expect to pay $2,000–$3,500 for a single-family purchase in NYC.

Required in NY — not optional Hire before making offers
Who to find: Ask your agent for referrals, or use the NY State Bar Association's referral service. Avoid attorneys who also represent sellers — conflicts happen.
4
Phase 2 · The search
Find a buyer's agent and search seriously

A buyer's agent costs you nothing — their commission (typically 2.5–3%) is paid by the seller. However, as of 2024, agent compensation rules have changed and you may need to sign a buyer's agreement upfront. Read it carefully.

In the outer boroughs, inventory for single-family homes is tight. Expect to see properties within hours of listing and bid within days. Set up real-time alerts on StreetEasy, Zillow, and Realtor.com. Drive the neighborhoods on weekends — some sellers list quietly through agents only.

Commission typically paid by seller StreetEasy dominates NYC Outer borough moves faster than Manhattan
5
Phase 2 · The search
Make an offer

When you find the right home, your agent submits an offer letter (not a formal contract yet) with your price, proposed closing timeline, and contingencies. In a competitive market, sellers may counter or run a "best and final" round.

Common offer contingencies: mortgage financing, home inspection, and clear title. Waiving contingencies makes your offer stronger but increases your risk. Never waive the inspection contingency on a house you haven't already inspected.

Keep financing contingency if possible Don't waive inspection blind Escalation clauses in bidding wars
NYC etiquette: Once verbally accepted, notify your attorney immediately — NY deals are informal until a signed contract exists, and sellers can accept another offer until then ("gazumping").
6
Phase 3 · Under contract
Home inspection + due diligence

Once your offer is accepted, schedule a licensed home inspector within days — before the contract is signed if possible. In NYC, inspectors also check for open permits, ECB violations (Environmental Control Board), HPD violations, and Department of Buildings liens. These can cost you thousands to resolve.

For older NYC homes, pay particular attention to: oil tank removal history, knob-and-tube wiring, cast iron plumbing, and the roof. A sewer scope ($200–$400 extra) is highly recommended for any house over 30 years old.

Home inspection$500–$900
Sewer scope (recommended)$200–$400
Radon test$150–$300
Oil tank sweep (if applicable)$200–$500
7
Phase 3 · Under contract
Sign the contract of sale + pay 10% deposit

Your attorney and the seller's attorney negotiate the contract of sale — this is where deal terms, inclusions (appliances, fixtures), closing date, and contingencies are formalized. Once both sides sign, you wire 10% of the purchase price as a deposit, held in the seller's attorney's escrow account.

At this point the deal is binding. If you back out without a valid contingency, you lose your deposit. If the seller backs out, they must return your deposit plus penalties (varies by contract).

For a $950K home: Your 10% contract deposit is $95,000 — wired to escrow. This counts toward your down payment at closing.
10% deposit goes at risk 2–4 weeks to negotiate contract
8
Phase 3 · Under contract
Apply for the mortgage (formally)

Now that you have a signed contract with an address, submit your full mortgage application. Your lender orders an appraisal (required for any loan) — if the home appraises below your purchase price, you may need to renegotiate or cover the gap in cash.

Respond to lender document requests immediately — delays here push your closing date. Avoid any major financial changes: don't quit your job, don't buy a car, don't open new credit cards.

Appraisal fee$500–$750
Loan origination fee (varies)0–1% of loan
Lock your rate — rates can move fast No major purchases or job changes
9
Phase 3 · Under contract
Title search + title insurance

A title company (coordinated by your attorney) searches public records to confirm the seller has clear ownership and there are no liens, unpaid taxes, judgments, or legal claims against the property. This takes 1–3 weeks.

You'll buy two title insurance policies: lender's (required by your bank) and owner's (optional but strongly recommended). Owner's title insurance is a one-time payment that protects you forever against future title claims.

Owner's title insurance (~0.5% of price)~$4,750
Lender's title insurance (often bundled)~$500–$900
10
Phase 4 · Closing
Final walkthrough + closing day

24–48 hours before closing, do a final walkthrough to confirm the home is in agreed-upon condition, agreed items are still there, and any inspection repairs were completed. Bring your checklist.

Closing happens at a title company or attorney's office — typically lasts 2–3 hours. You'll sign a large stack of documents. Your attorney handles the money flow: you wire your remaining down payment + closing costs the day before. The seller signs the deed over to you, and you get the keys.

NYC/NYS transfer tax (paid by buyer on new homes; seller on resale)1.825%
Mortgage recording tax1.8% of loan
Attorney fees$2,000–$3,500
Title + filing fees$1,500–$2,500
Mansion tax (if over $1M)1–3.9%
Wire fraud warning: Closing wire instructions are a top target for fraud. Always verify wiring details by calling your attorney directly using a number you looked up yourself — never trust instructions sent by email alone.
✓

Once the deed is recorded (usually within a week of closing), you're officially on title. A few immediate priorities:

NYC-specific things to know

  • Unlike most of the US, NY real estate deals aren't binding until both attorneys sign a formal contract. Verbal agreements mean nothing — a seller can legally accept another offer right up until ink dries.
  • Property taxes in NYC are notoriously low for Class 1 residential (single-family), often 0.5–0.9% effective rate, because assessed values are frozen far below market. This can change if the property is reassessed after sale.
  • The mansion tax kicks in at exactly $1,000,000 — buying at $999,999 saves you $10,000+. Many buyers negotiate to stay under this threshold.
  • Flood zone maps matter across NYC, especially in coastal areas of Queens, Brooklyn, and Staten Island. Check FEMA FIRM maps before buying — flood insurance can add $2,000–$8,000/yr and lenders require it if you're in a high-risk zone.
  • Zoning varies by neighborhood — check with the DOB before any additions or ADU plans. Accessory dwelling units have expanded allowances under recent NYC zoning changes.
  • Always search the NYC DOF ACRIS database (acris.nyc.gov) and DOB BIS (a-b-info.nyc.gov) for open violations and permits before making an offer — your attorney will do this but knowing yourself helps you ask better questions.
